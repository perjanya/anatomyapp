What are the five anatomical peculiarities of the clavicle?	1. Only long bone placed horizontally.<br>2. First bone to ossify in intrauterine life (5th-6th week).<br>3. Only long bone that ossifies predominantly by intramembranous ossification.<br>4. Only long bone having two primary ossification centres.<br>5. Pierced by cutaneous nerves (supraclavicular nerves).	UpperLimb::Osteology::Clavicle
At which site does the clavicle most commonly fracture, and why?	At the <b>junction of its medial two-thirds and lateral one-third</b>.<br>• It marks the junction between two opposing curvatures.<br>• It is the site of transmission of body weight from the clavicle to scapula via the coracoclavicular ligament.	UpperLimb::Osteology::Clavicle
How are the bone fragments displaced in a fracture of the clavicle?	• <b>Medial fragment:</b> Displaced superiorly/upward by the pull of the <b>sternocleidomastoid muscle</b>.<br>• <b>Lateral fragment:</b> Displaced downward by gravity/weight of the limb, and pulled medially by the <b>teres major and adductor muscles</b>.	UpperLimb::Osteology::Clavicle
Which neurovascular structures are most vulnerable to injury in a clavicular fracture?	<b>Subclavian vessels</b> (subclavian artery and vein) and the <b>medial cord of the brachial plexus</b>.	UpperLimb::Osteology::Clavicle
What muscular and ligamentous structures attach to the tip of the coracoid process?	• <b>Muscles (Origin):</b> Short head of biceps brachii and coracobrachialis.<br>• <b>Ligaments:</b> Coracoclavicular, coracoacromial, and coracohumeral ligaments.<br><i>(Note: Pectoralis minor inserts on its medial border/base).</i>	UpperLimb::Osteology::Scapula
Why is the coracoid process described as the 'lighthouse of the shoulder'?	Because it is a prominent, palpable landmark lying in close proximity to major neurovascular structures: the <b>brachial plexus</b>, <b>axillary artery</b>, and <b>axillary vein</b>.	UpperLimb::Osteology::Scapula
What type of epiphysis does the coracoid process represent?	An <b>atavistic epiphysis</b> (represents an independent bone in ancestral lower animals and birds that has fused with the scapula in mammals).	UpperLimb::Osteology::Scapula
What are the boundaries and contents of the Quadrangular Space?	• <b>Superior:</b> Teres minor (or subscapularis anteriorly)<br>• <b>Inferior:</b> Teres major<br>• <b>Medial:</b> Long head of triceps brachii<br>• <b>Lateral:</b> Surgical neck of humerus<br>• <b>Contents:</b> <b>Axillary nerve</b> and <b>posterior circumflex humeral vessels</b>.	UpperLimb::Spaces
What are the boundaries and contents of the Upper Triangular Space?	• <b>Superior:</b> Teres minor<br>• <b>Inferior:</b> Teres major<br>• <b>Lateral:</b> Long head of triceps brachii<br>• <b>Contents:</b> <b>Circumflex scapular artery</b>.	UpperLimb::Spaces
What are the boundaries and contents of the Lower Triangular Space?	• <b>Superior:</b> Teres major<br>• <b>Medial:</b> Long head of triceps brachii<br>• <b>Lateral:</b> Shaft of humerus<br>• <b>Contents:</b> <b>Radial nerve</b> and <b>profunda brachii vessels</b>.	UpperLimb::Spaces
Which arteries form the arterial anastomosis around the body of the scapula?	1. <b>Suprascapular artery</b> (from thyrocervical trunk of subclavian artery)<br>2. <b>Deep branch of transverse cervical / dorsal scapular artery</b> (from subclavian artery)<br>3. <b>Subscapular artery and its circumflex scapular branch</b> (from 3rd part of axillary artery).	UpperLimb::Vasculature
What are the muscular boundaries of the axilla?	• <b>Anterior wall:</b> Pectoralis major and minor, subclavius<br>• <b>Posterior wall:</b> Subscapularis (above), teres major and latissimus dorsi (below)<br>• <b>Medial wall:</b> Upper 4-5 ribs and intercostal muscles, covered by serratus anterior<br>• <b>Lateral wall:</b> Intertubercular sulcus of humerus with coracobrachialis and short head of biceps.	UpperLimb::Axilla
How is the axillary artery divided into three parts?	By the <b>pectoralis minor muscle</b> crossing anterior to it:<br>• <b>1st part:</b> Proximal (medial) to pectoralis minor (gives 1 branch: Superior thoracic artery).<br>• <b>2nd part:</b> Deep (posterior) to pectoralis minor (gives 2 branches: Thoracoacromial and Lateral thoracic arteries).<br>• <b>3rd part:</b> Distal (lateral) to pectoralis minor (gives 3 branches: Subscapular, Anterior circumflex humeral, Posterior circumflex humeral arteries).	UpperLimb::Vasculature::AxillaryArtery
How are the cords of the brachial plexus named in relation to the axillary artery?	They are named according to their relationship to the <b>second part of the axillary artery</b>:<br>• <b>Lateral cord:</b> Lies lateral to the artery.<br>• <b>Medial cord:</b> Lies medial to the artery.<br>• <b>Posterior cord:</b> Lies posterior to the artery.	UpperLimb::BrachialPlexus
How is the axillary vein formed and where does it terminate?	• <b>Formation:</b> Formed at the lower border of teres major by the union of the <b>two venae comitantes of the brachial artery</b> and the <b>basilic vein</b>.<br>• <b>Termination:</b> Continues as the <b>subclavian vein</b> at the outer (lateral) border of the first rib.	UpperLimb::Vasculature::Veins
What four structures pierce the clavipectoral fascia?	1. <b>Lateral pectoral nerve</b><br>2. <b>Thoracoacromial artery</b><br>3. <b>Cephalic vein</b><br>4. <b>Lymphatics</b> (draining infraclavicular/pectoral nodes to apical axillary nodes).	UpperLimb::Fascia
What is the axillary tail of Spence, and why is it surgically unique?	It is an extension of the upper outer quadrant of breast tissue that pierces the deep fascia and extends into the axilla through the foramen of Langer.<br><b>Significance:</b> It is the <b>only part of normal breast tissue lying deep to the deep fascia</b>.	UpperLimb::Breast
What anatomical structure causes skin dimpling/puckering in carcinoma of the breast?	Infiltration and secondary shortening/contraction of the <b>suspensory ligaments of Cooper</b> (connective tissue bands anchoring the breast parenchyma to the dermis).	UpperLimb::Breast
What is the anatomical mechanism behind peau d'orange appearance of the breast skin?	Obstruction of subcutaneous lymphatics by tumor cells causes <b>localized cutaneous edema</b>. The skin swells, but is tethered down at the openings of sweat glands and hair follicles, creating an orange-peel texture.	UpperLimb::Breast
What is the retromammary space, and what is its clinical significance?	A loose areolar tissue plane located between the deep layer of superficial fascia enclosing the breast and the deep pectoral fascia.<br>• Allows free physiological mobility of the breast.<br>• Obliterated ('fixity' to chest wall) when carcinoma invades the pectoral fascia.	UpperLimb::Breast
Name the five anatomical groups of axillary lymph nodes and their surgical levels.	• <b>Groups:</b> Anterior (pectoral), Posterior (scapular), Lateral (axillary vein), Central, and Apical (subclavicular).<br>• <b>Surgical Levels (relative to Pectoralis Minor):</b><br>&nbsp;&nbsp;- <b>Level I:</b> Lateral/inferior to pectoralis minor.<br>&nbsp;&nbsp;- <b>Level II:</b> Deep/behind pectoralis minor.<br>&nbsp;&nbsp;- <b>Level III:</b> Medial/superior to pectoralis minor up to lower border of clavicle.	UpperLimb::Lymphatics::Breast
What is a sentinel lymph node in breast cancer?	The <b>first lymph node</b> that receives lymphatic drainage directly from the primary tumor. If it is negative for tumor cells on biopsy, extensive axillary lymph node dissection can often be avoided.	UpperLimb::Lymphatics::Breast
What are the roots, trunks, divisions, and cords of the brachial plexus?	• <b>Roots:</b> Ventral rami of C5, C6, C7, C8, and T1.<br>• <b>Trunks:</b> Upper (C5-C6), Middle (C7), Lower (C8-T1).<br>• <b>Divisions:</b> Each trunk splits into anterior (flexor) and posterior (extensor) divisions.<br>• <b>Cords:</b><br>&nbsp;&nbsp;- Lateral cord: Anterior divisions of upper and middle trunks (C5, C6, C7).<br>&nbsp;&nbsp;- Medial cord: Anterior division of lower trunk (C8, T1).<br>&nbsp;&nbsp;- Posterior cord: Posterior divisions of all three trunks (C5-T1).	UpperLimb::BrachialPlexus
What is Erb's point and which nerves meet there?	A point on the upper trunk of the brachial plexus where <b>6 nerves meet</b>:<br>1. C5 root<br>2. C6 root<br>3. Suprascapular nerve<br>4. Nerve to subclavius<br>5. Anterior division of upper trunk<br>6. Posterior division of upper trunk.	UpperLimb::BrachialPlexus
What is the root involvement, paralyzed muscles, and attitude in Erb's palsy?	• <b>Root:</b> Upper trunk (<b>C5-C6</b>).<br>• <b>Paralyzed muscles:</b> Deltoid, supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, biceps brachii, brachialis, brachioradialis, supinator.<br>• <b>Deformity (Waiter's tip / Policeman's tip):</b> Arm hangs adducted and internally rotated; elbow extended; forearm pronated.	UpperLimb::BrachialPlexus::Lesions
What is the root involvement, deformity, and associated signs in Klumpke's palsy?	• <b>Root:</b> Lower trunk (<b>C8-T1</b>).<br>• <b>Deficit:</b> Paralysis of all intrinsic muscles of the hand (interossei, thenar/hypothenar, lumbricals) and long digital flexors.<br>• <b>Deformity:</b> <b>Claw hand</b> (MCP hyperextension, IP flexion).<br>• <b>Associated sign:</b> <b>Horner's syndrome</b> (ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis) due to disruption of T1 sympathetic outflow.	UpperLimb::BrachialPlexus::Lesions
Which four muscles form the rotator cuff (SITS), and what are their individual actions?	1. <b>Supraspinatus:</b> Initiates abduction (0°-15°).<br>2. <b>Infraspinatus:</b> Powerful lateral (external) rotation.<br>3. <b>Teres minor:</b> Lateral (external) rotation.<br>4. <b>Subscapularis:</b> Medial (internal) rotation and adduction.<br><i>All four dynamically stabilize the humeral head in the glenoid cavity.</i>	UpperLimb::RotatorCuff
Where is the rotator cuff structurally deficient, and what is the clinical consequence?	It is deficient <b>inferiorly</b>.<br>Consequently, the glenohumeral joint is most susceptible to <b>inferior and anterior dislocations</b>.	UpperLimb::RotatorCuff
Which rotator cuff muscle inserts into the lesser tubercle of the humerus?	<b>Subscapularis</b>.<br><i>(Supraspinatus, infraspinatus, and teres minor insert into the superior, middle, and inferior facets of the greater tubercle, respectively).</i>	UpperLimb::RotatorCuff
What is Painful Arc Syndrome?	Pain elicited during arm abduction between <b>60° and 120°</b>.<br><b>Mechanism:</b> The inflamed supraspinatus tendon and subacromial bursa are mechanically compressed beneath the rigid coracoacromial arch during this arc.	UpperLimb::Clinical::Shoulder
What are the Hill-Sachs and Bankart lesions in recurrent anterior shoulder dislocation?	• <b>Hill-Sachs lesion:</b> Compression fracture / defect of the posterolateral aspect of the humeral head caused by impaction against the anterior glenoid rim.<br>• <b>Bankart lesion:</b> Detachment or tear of the anterior-inferior glenoid labrum.	UpperLimb::Clinical::Shoulder
What is the nerve supply and functional actions of the three parts of the Deltoid?	• <b>Nerve:</b> Axillary nerve (C5, C6).<br>• <b>Anterior fibers (unipennate):</b> Flexion and medial rotation of arm.<br>• <b>Middle fibers (multipennate):</b> Principal abductor of arm (15°-90°).<br>• <b>Posterior fibers (unipennate):</b> Extension and lateral rotation of arm.	UpperLimb::Muscles::Shoulder
Where is an intramuscular injection safely given into the deltoid, and which nerve is at risk?	• <b>Safe site:</b> Upper part of the lower half of the deltoid (or 3-4 fingerbreadths below the acromion).<br>• <b>Nerve at risk:</b> Anterior branch of the <b>axillary nerve</b> as it curves around the surgical neck of the humerus.	UpperLimb::Clinical
What is the nerve supply and clinical presentation of Long Thoracic Nerve injury?	• <b>Nerve:</b> Long thoracic nerve of Bell (C5, C6, C7), running on the external surface of serratus anterior.<br>• <b>Clinical deformity:</b> <b>Winging of the scapula</b> (medial border and inferior angle project backward when pushing forward against resistance).	UpperLimb::Nerves::LongThoracic
What are the boundaries of the Triangle of Auscultation, and what is its clinical utility?	• <b>Medial:</b> Trapezius<br>• <b>Inferior / Lateral:</b> Latissimus dorsi<br>• <b>Lateral / Superior:</b> Medial border of scapula<br>• <b>Utility:</b> Respiratory breath sounds are heard exceptionally well with a stethoscope because it is an area relatively devoid of thick overlying muscles.	UpperLimb::Back
What is the dual innervation of the Trapezius muscle?	• <b>Motor:</b> Spinal accessory nerve (Cranial Nerve XI).<br>• <b>Sensory / Proprioception:</b> Ventral rami of C3 and C4.	UpperLimb::Muscles::Back
Which three nerves are in direct contact with the humerus and at risk during fractures?	1. <b>Axillary nerve:</b> Winding around the surgical neck.<br>2. <b>Radial nerve:</b> Traversing the radial (spiral) groove of the shaft.<br>3. <b>Ulnar nerve:</b> Running behind the medial epicondyle.	UpperLimb::Osteology::Humerus
Why does a midshaft humerus fracture causing radial nerve injury produce wrist drop but SPARE elbow extension?	Motor branches of the radial nerve to the long and medial heads of the <b>triceps brachii</b> arise proximally in the axilla, <b>before the radial nerve enters the spiral groove</b>.	UpperLimb::Nerves::Radial
What is the origin, insertion, and unique leverage action of the Biceps Brachii?	• <b>Origin:</b> Long head from supraglenoid tubercle (intracapsular); Short head from tip of coracoid process.<br>• <b>Insertion:</b> Posterior rough part of the radial tuberosity (separated from anterior part by a bursa), and into deep fascia via bicipital aponeurosis.<br>• <b>Leverage:</b> Insertion onto the posterior radial tuberosity gives it powerful leverage as the <b>chief supinator of the flexed forearm</b>.	UpperLimb::Muscles::Arm
Which muscle has a dual innervation from both the musculocutaneous and radial nerves?	<b>Brachialis</b> (medial part by musculocutaneous; inferolateral small portion by radial nerve).	UpperLimb::Muscles::Arm
What are the boundaries, floor, and roof of the Cubital Fossa?	• <b>Boundaries:</b> Superior = imaginary line connecting humeral epicondyles; Lateral = medial border of brachioradialis; Medial = lateral border of pronator teres.<br>• <b>Floor:</b> Brachialis (medially) and Supinator (laterally).<br>• <b>Roof:</b> Skin, superficial fascia (with median cubital vein, lateral/medial cutaneous nerves of forearm), and deep fascia with bicipital aponeurosis.	UpperLimb::CubitalFossa
What are the contents of the Cubital Fossa from medial to lateral?	Mnemonic <b>MBBR</b>:<br>1. <b>M</b>edian nerve<br>2. <b>B</b>rachial artery (and terminal branches)<br>3. <b>B</b>iceps brachii tendon<br>4. <b>R</b>adial nerve (in the cleft between brachialis and brachioradialis).	UpperLimb::CubitalFossa
Why is the Median Cubital Vein the preferred site for venipuncture?	1. It is large, superficial, and readily identifiable.<br>2. It is secured and prevented from rolling by fibrous strands anchoring it to the underlying <b>bicipital aponeurosis</b>.<br>3. The bicipital aponeurosis shields the deeper brachial artery and median nerve from accidental needle puncture.	UpperLimb::Clinical::Venipuncture
What structures create the carrying angle of the elbow?	The <b>medial flange of the trochlea of the humerus</b> projects 4°-8° lower than the lateral flange, angling the extended ulna laterally relative to the humerus (10°-15° in males, 15°-20° in females).	UpperLimb::ElbowJoint
What is the anatomical defect in 'Nursemaid's elbow' (radial head subluxation)?	In children under 5 years, sudden axial traction on a pronated forearm causes the small radial head to slip partially out of the lax <b>annular ligament</b>, trapping the ligament between the radial head and the capitulum.	UpperLimb::Clinical::Elbow
What is the primary difference in muscle action between Supinator and Biceps Brachii during supination?	• <b>Supinator:</b> Prime mover for slow, unresisted supination in all positions of the elbow (extended or flexed).<br>• <b>Biceps brachii:</b> Recruited during <b>rapid or resisted supination when the elbow is flexed</b> (due to its high physiological cross-sectional area).	UpperLimb::Biomechanics
List the 8 carpal bones from lateral to medial in proximal and distal rows.	Mnemonic: <i>She Looks Too Pretty, Try To Catch Her</i><br>• <b>Proximal row:</b> <b>S</b>caphoid, <b>L</b>unate, <b>T</b>riquetrum, <b>P</b>isiform.<br>• <b>Distal row:</b> <b>T</b>rapezium, <b>T</b>rapezoid, <b>C</b>apitate, <b>H</b>amate.	UpperLimb::Osteology::Carpus
Why is a scaphoid fracture prone to avascular necrosis (Preiser's disease)?	The blood supply enters the scaphoid at its <b>distal pole</b> and flows in a <b>retrograde direction</b> to the proximal pole. A fracture across the scaphoid waist interrupts this intraosseous arterial chain, leaving the proximal fragment devascularized.	UpperLimb::Clinical::Carpus
What are the boundaries and contents of the Anatomical Snuffbox?	• <b>Lateral / Radial boundary:</b> Tendons of Abductor pollicis longus (APL) and Extensor pollicis brevis (EPB).<br>• <b>Medial / Ulnar boundary:</b> Tendon of Extensor pollicis longus (EPL).<br>• <b>Floor:</b> Scaphoid and Trapezium.<br>• <b>Contents:</b> Radial artery, superficial branch of radial nerve, origin of cephalic vein.	UpperLimb::Hand::Snuffbox
Which 9 tendons and 1 nerve pass through the Carpal Tunnel?	• <b>Tendons (9):</b><br>&nbsp;&nbsp;- 4 tendons of Flexor Digitorum Superficialis (FDS)<br>&nbsp;&nbsp;- 4 tendons of Flexor Digitorum Profundus (FDP)<br>&nbsp;&nbsp;- 1 tendon of Flexor Pollicis Longus (FPL)<br>• <b>Nerve (1):</b> <b>Median nerve</b>.	UpperLimb::Hand::CarpalTunnel
What type of joint is the first carpometacarpal (trapeziometacarpal) joint?	A <b>saddle (sellar) synovial joint</b> between the trapezium and the base of the first metacarpal, allowing multiaxial movement including thumb opposition.	UpperLimb::Joints
Which single intrinsic muscle is the primary initiator of thumb opposition?	<b>Abductor pollicis brevis</b> (innervated by the recurrent branch of the median nerve).	UpperLimb::Hand::Muscles
What are the actions and mnemonics of the Interossei muscles of the hand?	• <b>PAD:</b> <b>P</b>almar interossei <b>AD</b>duct digits toward the midline (axis through 3rd digit).<br>• <b>DAB:</b> <b>D</b>orsal interossei <b>AB</b>duct digits away from the midline.<br><i>Both also assist lumbricals in MCP flexion and IP extension.</i>	UpperLimb::Hand::Muscles
What is the unique action of the Lumbrical muscles of the hand?	They produce <b>flexion of the metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joints</b> and simultaneous <b>extension of the interphalangeal (IP) joints</b> (via their insertions into the extensor expansions).	UpperLimb::Hand::Muscles
Why are the midpalmar/thenar spaces termed 'pus tanks' and lumbrical canals termed 'pus taps'?	• <b>Pus tanks:</b> Deep potential spaces (midpalmar and thenar) where large collections of pus accumulate in severe deep hand infections.<br>• <b>Pus taps:</b> The <b>lumbrical canals</b> communicate proximally with these spaces and open distally into the web spaces, acting as drainage conduits.	UpperLimb::Hand::Spaces
What is a 'collar-button' (hourglass) abscess of the hand?	An abscess that spans the web space of the hand, consisting of a deep collection beneath the palmar aponeurosis connected through a narrow neck to a superficial subcutaneous collection in the interdigital web.	UpperLimb::Clinical::Hand
What is the difference between Paronychia and Felon (Whitlow)?	• <b>Paronychia:</b> Infection involving the nail fold / eponychium.<br>• <b>Felon (Whitlow):</b> Closed-space infection of the pulp space of the distal phalanx; increasing pressure can cause ischemic necrosis of the distal bone shaft.	UpperLimb::Clinical::Hand
How is the Superficial Palmar Arch formed, and what are its main branches?	• <b>Formation:</b> Primarily formed by the terminal continuation of the <b>ulnar artery</b>, completed on the radial side by the superficial palmar branch of the radial artery.<br>• <b>Branches:</b> 3 common palmar digital arteries and 1 proper digital artery to the medial side of the little finger.	UpperLimb::Vasculature::Hand
How is the Deep Palmar Arch formed, and what are its main branches?	• <b>Formation:</b> Formed by the terminal continuation of the <b>radial artery</b>, completed on the ulnar side by the deep palmar branch of the ulnar artery.<br>• <b>Branches:</b> 3 palmar metacarpal arteries, 3 perforating branches, and recurrent branches.	UpperLimb::Vasculature::Hand
How is Allen's Test interpreted before radial artery cannulation or harvesting?	Both radial and ulnar arteries are compressed while the patient clenches their fist to blanch the hand; ulnar pressure is then released:<br>• <b>Positive (Normal):</b> Hand pinks up within 5-15 seconds (adequate ulnar collateral circulation).<br>• <b>Negative (Abnormal):</b> Hand remains pale >15 seconds (insufficient ulnar collateral; radial artery must NOT be punctured or harvested).	UpperLimb::Clinical::Vasculature
What are the motor deficits and clinical tests for Median Nerve injury?	• <b>Pen test:</b> Inability to touch a pen held above the palm due to paralyzed <b>abductor pollicis brevis</b>.<br>• <b>Ochsner's clasp test:</b> Index and middle fingers fail to flex when hands are clasped together (paralyzed FDS & lateral FDP).<br>• <b>Hand of Benediction / Pointing index:</b> Index/middle fingers remain extended upon attempting to make a fist.	UpperLimb::Nerves::Median
What are the boundaries and contents of Guyon's Canal (pisohamate canal)?	• <b>Medial:</b> Pisiform bone.<br>• <b>Lateral:</b> Hook of hamate.<br>• <b>Roof:</b> Palmar carpal ligament and palmaris brevis.<br>• <b>Floor:</b> Flexor retinaculum.<br>• <b>Contents:</b> <b>Ulnar nerve</b> (dividing into superficial and deep branches) and <b>ulnar artery</b>.	UpperLimb::Hand::GuyonCanal
What is Froment's Paper Sign, and what muscle compensates for the deficit?	Tests for ulnar nerve palsy. When holding a piece of paper between the thumb and index finger against resistance, paralysis of <b>adductor pollicis</b> forces the patient to strongly flex the thumb interphalangeal joint using the <b>flexor pollicis longus (median nerve)</b>.	UpperLimb::Clinical::Ulnar
Explain the anatomical basis of the 'Ulnar Paradox'.	An ulnar nerve injury at the <b>wrist produces MORE pronounced clawing</b> than an injury at the elbow.<br><b>Reason:</b> An injury at the elbow also paralyzes the ulnar half of the <b>flexor digitorum profundus (FDP)</b>. Without active profundus tendon pull, the distal IP joints cannot flex, making the claw deformity appear less severe.	UpperLimb::Nerves::Ulnar
What are the autonomous sensory testing zones for the Median, Ulnar, and Radial nerves in the hand?	• <b>Median nerve:</b> Palmar aspect of the tip of the index finger.<br>• <b>Ulnar nerve:</b> Palmar and dorsal aspects of the tip of the little finger.<br>• <b>Radial nerve:</b> Dorsal surface of the first web space between the thumb and index finger.	UpperLimb::Nerves::Cutaneous
