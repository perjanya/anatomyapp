SECTION I: Structured Clinical Cases (10 Marks Each)

Case 1: Breast Carcinoma & Axillary Clearance

A 52-year-old female presents to the surgical outpatient department with a hard, painless lump in the upper outer quadrant of her right breast. On clinical examination, the overlying skin exhibits a dimpled, orange-peel (peau d’orange) appearance, and a hard, matted lymph node is palpated in the right axilla.

  1. Name the quadrants of the breast and identify the one most commonly affected by carcinoma. (1 mark)
  2. Explain the anatomical basis for:
    1. Dimpling / puckering of the overlying skin. (1 mark)
    2. Peau d’orange appearance. (1 mark)
  3. Describe the lymphatic drainage of the breast under:
    1. Primary axillary lymph node groups. (2 marks)
    2. Surgical levels (Berg’s levels) in relation to the pectoralis minor muscle. (2 marks)
  4. If a radical mastectomy with axillary dissection is performed, identify two major nerves at risk in the axilla and describe the resulting clinical deformities if injured. (3 marks)

Case 2: Anterior Dislocation of the Shoulder Joint

A 24-year-old rugby player falls onto his abducted and laterally rotated right arm. He presents to the casualty supporting his right forearm with his left hand, exhibiting loss of the normal rounded contour of the right shoulder and a prominent acromion process.

  1. What is the probable diagnosis? (1 mark)
  2. Describe the shoulder (glenohumeral) joint under:
    1. Articulating surfaces and type of joint. (1.5 marks)
    2. Factors providing dynamic and static stability. (2.5 marks)
  3. Explain why the glenohumeral joint is structurally predisposed to dislocate in an anteroinferior direction. (2 marks)
  4. Which nerve is most vulnerable to traction or compression in this dislocation? Describe its motor and sensory testing. (3 marks)

Case 3: Upper Brachial Plexus Injury (Erb's Palsy)

A newborn infant delivered by difficult breech extraction presents with an asymmetrical posture of the right upper limb. The arm hangs limp by the side of the trunk, adducted and internally rotated, with the elbow fully extended and the forearm pronated.

  1. Identify the clinical condition and the exact site of injury in the brachial plexus. (1 mark)
  2. Name the spinal roots involved and define Erb’s point with the six nerves that meet there. (3 marks)
  3. Provide the anatomical explanation for each component of the deformity:
    1. Adduction of the arm (1 mark)
    2. Medial rotation of the arm (1 mark)
    3. Extension of the elbow (1 mark)
    4. Pronation of the forearm (1 mark)
  4. What is this characteristic clinical posture colloquially termed? (2 marks)

Case 4: Midshaft Humeral Fracture & Radial Nerve Palsy

A 32-year-old motorcyclist sustains a closed fracture of the middle third of his right humeral shaft. Following splinting, he is unable to extend his right wrist or fingers, and he complains of numbness over the dorsum of the first web space.

  1. Identify the nerve injured and the accompanying blood vessel at risk at this fracture site. (1.5 marks)
  2. Describe the radial (spiral) groove of the humerus under: boundaries, course, and branches given within the groove. (4.5 marks)
  3. Explain why this patient exhibits classic wrist drop yet retains active extension at the elbow joint. (2.5 marks)
  4. Describe the autonomous sensory zone used to clinically test this nerve. (1.5 marks)

Case 5: Supracondylar Fracture of the Humerus & Vascular Compromise

An 8-year-old boy falls onto his outstretched hand with his elbow hyperextended while playing in a playground. He presents with severe swelling and deformity above the elbow. Radial pulse at the wrist is faint, and passive finger extension causes excruciating pain.

  1. Mention four anatomical factors that predispose the supracondylar region of the humerus to fracture in children. (2 marks)
  2. Identify the major nerve and blood vessel most vulnerable to injury by the anteriorly displaced proximal fracture fragment. (2 marks)
  3. Explain the pathomechanics of Volkmann’s ischaemic contracture if the vascular compromise is left untreated. (3 marks)
  4. Explain how the geometry of the three bony points of the elbow (olecranon, medial epicondyle, and lateral epicondyle) differentiates a supracondylar fracture from a posterior dislocation of the elbow. (3 marks)

Case 6: Deep Palmar Space Infection (Midpalmar Space Abscess)

A 45-year-old manual laborer sustains a puncture wound from a rusty wire in his palm. Four days later, he presents with throbbing pain, loss of the normal hollow of the palm, marked swelling over the dorsum of the hand, and tenderness on the medial side of the hand.

  1. Which deep fascial space of the hand is primarily infected? (1 mark)
  2. Describe the boundaries (superficial, deep, medial, and lateral) and contents of this space. (4 marks)
  3. Explain:
    1. Why marked edema appears on the dorsum of the hand rather than the palm. (1.5 marks)
    2. How infection from this space can track distally into the web spaces to form a "collar-button" abscess. (2 marks)
  4. Name the proximal space in the forearm with which this palmar space communicates. (1.5 marks)

Case 7: Compression in Guyon’s Canal & Ulnar Nerve Neuropathy

A 28-year-old long-distance cyclist complains of tingling and progressive numbness in his right little finger and the medial half of his ring finger. On physical examination, he cannot hold a sheet of paper between his extended thumb and index finger without flexing the terminal phalanx of his thumb.

  1. Name the anatomical canal compressed and enumerate its boundaries and contents. (3.5 marks)
  2. Name the bedside clinical test described with the sheet of paper and explain its anatomical basis (Froment’s sign). (2.5 marks)
  3. Enumerate the intrinsic muscles of the hand innervated by the deep branch of the ulnar nerve. (3 marks)
  4. What is the autonomous sensory testing area for the ulnar nerve? (1 mark)

Case 8: Carpal Tunnel Syndrome

A 48-year-old computer typist presents with burning pain, tingling, and numbness in her lateral three and a half digits that frequently wakes her up at night. Examination reveals wasting of the thenar eminence and weakness of thumb opposition.

  1. What is the clinical diagnosis? (1 mark)
  2. Describe the carpal tunnel under:
    1. Bony attachments of the flexor retinaculum (transverse carpal ligament). (2.5 marks)
    2. Exact enumerations of all tendons and nerves passing through it. (3.5 marks)
  3. Explain why sensation over the central palm and skin overlying the thenar eminence is characteristically spared in carpal tunnel syndrome. (2 marks)
  4. Name and describe one specific motor test to confirm thenar muscle weakness due to median nerve compression. (1 mark)

Case 9: Scaphoid Fracture & Avascular Necrosis

A 22-year-old student falls heavily onto her outstretched right hand. Radiographs immediately after the fall do not show a definite fracture line, but she exhibits marked, pinpoint tenderness on deep pressure inside the anatomical snuffbox.

  1. Which bone is suspected to be fractured? (1 mark)
  2. Enumerate the boundaries (medial, lateral) and contents of the anatomical snuffbox. (3.5 marks)
  3. Explain the vascular pattern of this bone and why fractures through its waist carry an exceptionally high risk of avascular necrosis and non-union. (3.5 marks)
  4. Why is a repeat radiograph routinely recommended after 10–14 days if initial films appear normal? (2 marks)

Case 10: Axillary Artery Occlusion & Scapular Collateral Circulation

A 60-year-old man undergoes ligation of the first part of the axillary artery following a traumatic penetrating injury to the axillary base. Remarkably, the distal upper limb remains warm with palpable radial and ulnar pulses.

  1. What anatomical network enables bypass of this arterial obstruction? (1.5 marks)
  2. Describe the arterial anastomosis around the scapula under:
    1. Branches derived from the subclavian artery. (2.5 marks)
    2. Branches derived from the axillary artery. (2.5 marks)
  3. Trace the precise route of collateral arterial flow from the subclavian artery to the third part of the axillary artery. (2.5 marks)
  4. What clinical sign (pseudo-fracture) can appear on the lateral border of the scapula on an X-ray due to chronically enlarged collateral vessels? (1 mark)

  1. Why does an infection in the metaphysis of a long bone (haematogenous osteomyelitis) rarely spread directly into the adjacent joint cavity in young children?
  2. Why is the medial segment of a fractured clavicle displaced upward, while the lateral segment is displaced downward and medially?
  3. Why is the coracoid process described as the "lighthouse of the shoulder"?
  4. Why does carcinomatous invasion of the retromammary space cause "fixity" of the breast to the chest wall?
  5. Why is the median cubital vein preferred over other superficial veins of the upper limb for routine venipuncture?
  6. Why does sudden rupture of the supraspinatus tendon cause immediate inability to initiate smooth arm abduction?
  7. Explain the anatomical basis of "Painful Arc Syndrome" between 60° and 120° of arm abduction.
  8. Why is an intramuscular injection into the deltoid given specifically in the upper part of its lower half? Which nerve is at risk?
  9. Why does injury to the long thoracic nerve of Bell lead to "winging of the scapula"?
  10. *Explain why a high lesion of the ulnar nerve at the elbow produces a LESS severe claw hand deformity than a low lesion at the wrist (Ulnar Paradox).*
  11. Why is elbow extension preserved in radial nerve injuries occurring within the spiral groove of the humerus?
  12. Why does a modified Allen’s test showing pallor for more than 15 seconds contraindicate the harvest of the radial artery for coronary bypass surgery?
  13. *Why does a toddler pulled sharply by the hand with the forearm pronated develop subluxation of the radial head (nursemaid’s elbow)?*
  14. Why is the carrying angle of the elbow greater in females than in males, and what bony feature creates it?
  15. Why does a patient with median nerve compression in the carpal tunnel retain normal cutaneous sensation over the thenar eminence?
  16. *Why does a patient with an ulnar nerve lesion at the wrist show compensatory hyperflexion of the thumb interphalangeal joint when gripping a card (Froment’s sign)?*
  17. Why does lateral tennis elbow cause localized tenderness over the lateral epicondyle of the humerus during resisted wrist extension?
  18. Why are screws tightened using clockwise motion for a right-handed person? Explain on the basis of supinator biomechanics.
  19. Why does an undisplaced transverse fracture of the scaphoid waist have a high propensity to develop non-union and avascular necrosis of its proximal pole?
