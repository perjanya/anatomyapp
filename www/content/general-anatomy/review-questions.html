Review questions

Instructions: Select the one best answer for each question.

1. A CT image shows a horizontal section through the abdomen, dividing the body into upper and lower parts. Which anatomical plane does this represent?

A. Coronal plane
B. Median plane
C. Transverse plane
D. Parasagittal plane

2. During examination, a patient moves the upper limb sideways, away from the trunk. What is this movement called?

A. Abduction
B. Adduction
C. Flexion
D. Medial rotation

3. A wound is described as being on the forearm, closer to the elbow than the wrist. Which term best describes its position relative to the wrist?

A. Superficial
B. Distal
C. Medial
D. Proximal

4. The alveoli of the lungs have a very thin lining that allows rapid gas exchange. Which epithelial type is best suited to this function?

A. Stratified squamous epithelium
B. Simple squamous epithelium
C. Simple cuboidal epithelium
D. Transitional epithelium

5. A tissue sample contains cells scattered within an abundant extracellular matrix containing fibres and ground substance. Which basic tissue does this description identify?

A. Connective tissue
B. Epithelial tissue
C. Muscle tissue

6. A tendon is adapted to resist strong pulling forces mainly along its length. Which arrangement best explains this property?

A. A network of branching elastic fibres
B. Closely packed epithelial cells

B. Growth of the bone in length

Answers and explanations

No.

Answer

Explanation

1

C — Transverse plane

A transverse plane divides the body into superior and inferior parts.

2

A — Abduction

Abduction moves a body part away from the median plane.

3

D — Proximal

Proximal means nearer the attachment or root of a limb.

4

B — Simple squamous epithelium

A single layer of flattened cells provides a thin surface for diffusion.

5

A — Connective tissue

An abundant extracellular matrix is a characteristic feature of connective tissue.

6

C — Parallel bundles of collagen fibres

Tendons contain dense regular connective tissue adapted to tensile forces along the fibre direction.

7

B — Growth of the bone in length

Longitudinal growth depends on cartilage growth and its replacement by bone at the epiphyseal plate.

8

D — Diaphysis

The primary ossification centre usually develops in the shaft; secondary centres usually develop in the epiphyses.

9

A — Connective tissue covering versus bone

The periosteum is a covering with an inner osteogenic layer. The bone collar is bone deposited around the shaft of the cartilage model.

10

C — Metaphysis

The arrangement of metaphyseal vessels, including slow flow in terminal vascular loops, favours localisation of blood-borne bacteria in children.

11

D — Osteoprogenitor cell → osteoblast → osteocyte

Osteoblasts form bone matrix. Those enclosed within the matrix differentiate into osteocytes.

12

B — Sesamoid bone

Sesamoid bones develop within tendons. The patella also improves the mechanical advantage of the quadriceps.

13

C — Absence of blood vessels

Articular cartilage is avascular and lacks perichondrium, limiting its capacity for repair.

14

A — Fibrocartilage

Fibrocartilage in the annulus fibrosus helps resist tensile and compressive stresses.

15

D — Limited capacity to stretch

Firm fascial boundaries restrict expansion. Rising pressure can compromise tissue perfusion and nerve function.

16

B — Retinaculum

A retinaculum is a local thickening of deep fascia that holds tendons close to underlying structures.

17

A — The epidermis contains no blood vessels

The epidermis receives nutrients by diffusion from vessels in the underlying dermis.

18

C — Reduced separation of wound edges

Incisions parallel to skin tension lines generally gape less and may produce less conspicuous scars.

19

D — A joint cavity enclosed by a capsule

A synovial cavity is the defining feature. The articulating surfaces are covered by articular cartilage.

20

B — Saddle joint

Its reciprocally concave and convex surfaces allow movement in two principal planes.

21

A — Secondary cartilaginous joint

A symphysis joins bones through fibrocartilage, with hyaline cartilage covering the opposing bony surfaces.

22

C — Arterial anastomoses

Connections between arteries can provide alternative routes for blood flow.

23

D — Valves

Venous valves limit backward flow; skeletal muscle contractions help propel blood towards the heart.

24

B — Ischaemia with a risk of infarction

Without an effective alternative supply, arterial obstruction deprives tissue of oxygen and may cause tissue death.

25

A — More fibres and greater force production

Pennation permits a larger physiological cross-sectional area within a given muscle volume, favouring force production.

26

C — Triceps brachii

Triceps extends the elbow and therefore opposes elbow flexion.

27

B — Perineurium

Perineurium surrounds a fascicle; endoneurium surrounds individual nerve fibres, and epineurium surrounds the whole nerve.

28

D — Mixed nerve

A mixed nerve contains both sensory and motor fibres.

29

A — Impaired lymphatic drainage

Disruption of lymphatic pathways allows interstitial fluid and proteins to accumulate, producing lymphoedema.

30

C — Overlapping endothelial cells

Their overlapping edges permit fluid entry when interstitial pressure exceeds pressure within the lymphatic capillary.
